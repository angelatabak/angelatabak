Armed with exactly one week of label knowledge, I bought a bottle marked **+5** on the *nihonshu-dō* — the "sake meter value" that every beginner guide presents as the dryness scale. Positive means dry, negative means sweet, and +5 is comfortably into dry territory. I chilled it, poured it, and tasted something round, gentle and — there's no other word — sweet.

The label wasn't lying. I was just asking the number a question it never promised to answer.

## What the meter actually measures

The nihonshu-dō is a **density reading**. Take the finished sake, compare its density to pure water, and note which way it leans. Sugar is heavier than water, so residual sugar drags the value negative; alcohol is lighter, so a drier, more fully fermented sake floats the value positive. The everyday range runs from about **−15 (dense, sugar-rich)** to **+15 (light, bone-dry)**, with most bottles clustering between −3 and +10.

So the meter does track sugar — genuinely. If that were all there was to perception, the guides would be right and my +5 would have tasted austere.

> **The idea:** nihonshu-dō measures how much sugar is *in the sake*. It cannot measure how sweet the sake *feels* — because your tongue doesn't taste sugar in isolation.

## The two numbers the meter can't see

**Acidity** — *sandō* on the label — is the big one. Acidity reads as dryness: it tightens a sake, sharpens its edges, makes it finish clean. A sake with lively acidity tastes drier than its sugar content says it should; a sake with very low acidity tastes softer and sweeter than its meter value suggests. My round, gentle +5? Low acidity. All that "dryness" had nothing to push against, so what came through was softness — which the brain happily files as sweet. (Typical sandō runs from about 1.0 to 2.0; higher is brighter and more cutting.)

**Amino acids** — *aminosan-do*, listed less often — add a second layer: breadth, savouriness, a kind of mouth-filling richness that also blurs the impression of dryness.

Put differently: the meter measures the sake. Your tongue measures the *relationships* inside it. A +2 with high acidity can taste crisper than a +8 without it. One number was never going to carry that.

## How to actually use it

I haven't stopped reading the nihonshu-dō — I've stopped reading it alone. As a pair, the meter and the acidity tell you most of what one number pretended to:

- **High meter, high acidity:** genuinely dry, crisp, cutting. What most people mean by "dry."
- **High meter, low acidity:** technically dry, tastes gentle — my +5 lives here.
- **Low meter, high acidity:** sweet-and-bright, like good Riesling energy.
- **Low meter, low acidity:** openly plush and dessert-leaning.

If the label lists only the meter value, treat it as a **leaning, not a verdict** — and when a shop assistant asks what you like, "dry finish" or "soft finish" will get you further than any number.

There's a satisfying irony here: the previous post in this series was about a number that means the opposite of what you'd guess. This one is a number that means exactly what it says — about the liquid, not about you. Sake labels aren't misleading; they're just answering precise questions nobody told us how to phrase.
