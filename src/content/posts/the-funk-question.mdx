Two bottles, side by side at a tasting in Amsterdam. The first: melon, white flowers, almost transparent elegance. The second: yogurt, mushroom broth, something between leather and fresh bread. Same country, same grain, both unmistakably sake. My honest first note on the second one was a question — *"is this supposed to taste like this?"*

It is. And once I understood where that wildness comes from, it went from the sake I was politely tolerating to the one I keep buying.

## Every sake starts with a bodyguard problem

Before a brewery can ferment a full tank, it needs a **starter** — a small, dense culture of yeast (*shubo* or *moto*, "the mother") strong enough to take over the main mash. But a young starter is vulnerable: the tank is full of sugar-rich rice, and wild bacteria would love to move in first and spoil everything.

The bodyguard is **lactic acid**. Make the starter acidic enough and almost nothing harmful survives, while the sake yeast — which tolerates acid — thrives. Every brewery needs that acid. The entire divide between "orchard" sake and "farmhouse" sake comes down to **where the acid comes from**.

> **The idea:** modern breweries *add* lactic acid and are done in two weeks. Traditional methods *grow* it, using wild bacteria, over about four — and those extra weeks of microbial life leave flavours you can taste.

## The fast way and the slow way

The modern method, **sokujo** ("quick brew," early 1900s), simply adds food-grade lactic acid on day one. The starter is safe from the first hour, ready in roughly two weeks, and — crucially — *clean*. Nothing lived in that tank except the yeast the brewer chose. Most sake today is made this way, including nearly all the delicate ginjo styles from the seimaibuai post.

The old way, **kimoto** (codified around 1700), adds nothing. Brewery workers mash rice into a paste — traditionally by ramming it with poles through cold nights — and then *wait* for wild lactic-acid bacteria from the brewery itself to colonise the tank, produce acid, and then die off in the acid they made, clearing the stage for the yeast. It takes around **four weeks**, roughly double sokujo, and demands real skill: the brewer is conducting a succession of microbes rather than sterilising a lane for one.

**Yamahai** (1909) is kimoto's slightly younger sibling — researchers realised the pole-mashing wasn't microbiologically necessary and dropped it, keeping the wild lactic route. On a European import label, *kimoto* or *yamahai* in romaji is your signal.

## What the extra two weeks taste like

All that invisible life leaves fingerprints. More acidity, and a lactic kind of it — hence yogurt. More amino acids — hence the savoury broth-like depth, the umami. And a general widening of the flavour spectrum away from pure fruit: mushroom, grain, game, cream. Tasters reach for words like *earthy*, *gamey*, *wild*. "Funk" covers it nicely.

Two practical consequences. First, these sakes are usually **magnificent warmed** — heat amplifies the savoury side the way it flatters a good broth. Second, they are built for the table: all that acid and umami has enough muscle to stand next to serious food, where a whisper-delicate daiginjo would vanish. ## Choosing your side, or not

None of this is a quality ranking. Sokujo isn't a shortcut in the pejorative sense — it's precision, and the ethereal styles depend on it. Kimoto and yamahai aren't nostalgia — they're a different instrument, played slower. The label just lets you pick which concert you're attending: see *ginjo* or *daiginjo*, expect the orchard; see *kimoto* or *yamahai*, expect the farmhouse, and consider warming it.

My second bottle at that tasting — the one that made me ask if it was broken? A yamahai junmai. These days it's what I open when there's actual dinner on the table.
