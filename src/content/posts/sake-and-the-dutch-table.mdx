Every sake pairing guide I found when I started reads as if we all live ten minutes from Tsukiji market: sashimi, tempura, grilled eel. Lovely — and useless on a Tuesday in Amsterdam, where my actual shopping basket holds old cheese, things from an Indonesian toko, and herring when the season's right.

So this post does what the whole series has been building toward. Three posts ago: **polishing** decides delicate versus full (*seimaibuai*). Two posts ago: **acidity**, not the dryness number, decides how a sake cuts (*sandō*). Last post: **kimoto and yamahai** bring acid *and* umami — table muscle. That's the entire toolkit. Four working principles fall out of it: match intensity, let acid cut fat, stack umami on umami, and cool spice with sweetness. Five pairings, all from shops I actually walk past:

## 1. Three-year-old Gouda × yamahai junmai, gently warmed

The one that converted my sceptical friends. Aged Gouda is an umami monument — those crunchy crystals are the same savoury amino territory a yamahai swims in. This is **umami stacking**: the sake's broth-like depth and the cheese's caramel-savoury intensity amplify each other instead of competing. A delicate daiginjo would be flattened here (that's the intensity rule); the farmhouse style meets the farmhouse cheese as an equal. Warm the sake slightly — think tea-warm, not hot — and it opens further.

## 2. New herring with onions × a crisp, dry honjozo

*Hollandse nieuwe* is rich, oily, faintly sweet raw fish — and the classic partners (jenever, cold pils) already tell you what it wants: something clean with **cutting power**. A modest, well-made honjozo with decent acidity, served cold, slices through the fat and resets your mouth between bites. No perfume needed, no complexity contest — this is the *cleanse* principle doing quiet work. The humblest bottle on this list, and possibly the most reliable match.

## 3. Rendang × nigori

Indonesian-Dutch food is the Netherlands' great table tradition, and rendang — slow, rich, chilli-deep — bullies most wine into submission. Enter *nigori*, the cloudy style this site is named after: unfiltered, creamy, usually leaning sweet. Capsaicin heat is soothed by exactly two things nigori has in abundance — **sweetness and texture**. The rice-cream body coats, the gentle sugar cools, and the pairing works the way a lassi works with a curry. This is *complement*, not contrast: matching the dish's richness with richness of a different kind.

## 4. Bitterballen × sparkling sake

Deep-fried, molten, salty croquettes want what champagne gives croquettes: **scrub**. Sparkling sake — a young, often lightly sweet style with real fizz — does the same job with softer edges: carbonation lifts the fat, a touch of sweetness flatters the salt, and the low alcohol keeps it borrel-friendly. Frivolous? Slightly. So are bitterballen. Intensity matched.

## 5. A charcuterie board × kimoto junmai at room temperature

Cured meat is salt, fat and concentrated umami — which reads like a kimoto's dating profile. The wild-starter acidity cuts the fat (rule two), the amino depth shakes hands with the cure (rule three), and at room temperature — *hiya*, as a label might say — it behaves like a structured red wine without the tannin fights salume can pick. If you try only one pairing from this post with guests, make it this or the Gouda.

Notice what did all the work here: not tasting-note poetry, just **three label concepts** — polishing level, acidity, starter method — pointed at food. That's the bet this site is making: understand five numbers and you can build your own pairings from whatever your local shops sell, no sushi counter required.
