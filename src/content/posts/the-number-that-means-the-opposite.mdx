The first sake label I ever tried to decode had a proud little "23%" on it. I did what I suspect most people do: I assumed it was telling me how much of the rice had been polished away. Twenty-three percent milled off, seventy-seven left. A light touch. Artisanal restraint, probably.

It's the exact opposite. That bottle had **77% of every grain ground away**. Only 23% of each rice kernel — the very core — went into the tank.

The number is called *seimaibuai*, usually translated as "polishing ratio," and it answers one question: **how much of the grain remains.** Not how much was removed. A seimaibuai of 70 means a gentle polish and most of the grain intact. A seimaibuai of 23 means the brewery milled each grain down to a fraction of its original size before brewing even began.

> **The idea:** seimaibuai is the percentage of the rice grain that *survives* polishing. Lower number = more ground away = less of the grain in your glass.

## Why anyone would grind away 77% of perfectly good rice

Rice grains aren't uniform. The outer layers carry fats, proteins and minerals — nutritious in a rice bowl, troublesome in a fermentation tank. They ferment roughly, and they can drag rustic, cereal-heavy flavours into the sake. The centre of a good brewing grain, meanwhile, is a packed heart of nearly pure starch (brewers call it the *shinpaku*, the "white heart").

Polish away the outside and you're left with clean starch that ferments precisely and quietly. The result tends toward the delicate and perfumed: pear, melon, white flowers — the aromas people describe in top-end bottles. That style has a name you've seen on labels: sakes polished to 60% or below may be called *ginjo*, and 50% or below *daiginjo*. The 23% bottle that confused me sits at the extreme, indulgent end of daiginjo territory.

## Lower is not "better." It's a direction.

Here's where the number gets genuinely useful, and where the marketing gets ahead of itself. Heavy polishing removes the things that cause rough flavours — but those same layers are where a lot of sake's **body and umami** come from. Grind everything away and you gain elegance; you also give up savoury depth.

So read seimaibuai as a **style dial, not a score**:

- **High number (70–90%):** more of the grain, more grip — fuller, ricier, often more savoury. Wonderful with food.
- **Middle (50–60%):** the ginjo zone — aroma starts to bloom, still some body.
- **Low (23–50%):** the daiginjo zone — delicate, aromatic, precise. Often lovely alone, and easily bullied by strong dishes.

A brewery choosing 80% isn't being lazy, and one choosing 23% isn't automatically making something you'll enjoy more. They're aiming at different targets — and now you can see the target from the shelf.

## At the shop, in one move

Find the seimaibuai (it's on most labels, sometimes only on the back, written as 精米歩合 followed by the percentage — the number is all you need to spot). Then flip its meaning the way I had to: **read "23%" as "77% gone."** Small number, delicate and aromatic; big number, full and savoury. That single inversion puts you ahead of most people standing in front of a sake shelf in Europe.
